# 03 · Colour, materials, forms, composition — compatibility — durability
Rulebook v1.0 (2026-11-27). Manual v1.0: ch. 5, 7, 11. Tags: see `../design-interiores.md`.

## 5. Colour, materials, forms and composition
The composition builds balance, depth and a recognisable identity. Formulas help to start; look at visual weight, light
and the relations between elements.

### 5.1 Colour split 60/30/10 [EST]
- A composition tool, not a formula: about 60 % base, 30 % secondary colour or material, 10 % accent — may become
  70/20/10 or a near-monochrome scheme.
  - Base: large surfaces — walls, floor, ceiling, big visual planes.
  - Secondary: large furniture, joinery, curtains, large textiles.
  - Accent: art, cushions, objects, a special chair, small contrasting elements.
- Read the percentages as approximate visual weight, not measured area.
- Monochrome interiors can be refined when the variation comes from textures, finishes, relief and close tones; never
  force colour accents just to meet the formula.
- (DC) Materials per style: 3–4 is a reference, not a limit — user 2026-11-26 «nao restrinjas a 3 e 4 materiais porque há
  combinações que ficam com bem com mais materiais do que 3 ou 4, podemos manter 3 ou 4 como referência mas não algo
  obrigatório».

### 5.2 Whites, neutrals and undertones
- [EST] Avoid warm and cold whites clashing, but one single white for the whole house is not needed: slightly different
  whites in different rooms work if they belong to one coherent family and the differences are intentional.
- [REC] Identify the undertone of whites and neutrals: warm, cold, neutral, pinkish, yellowish, greenish, greyish.
- [REC] Judge colours next to the real materials and under the intended light, never from isolated digital swatches.
- [REC] Avoid a white looking accidentally yellow next to a bluish one.
- [CTX] Sun orientation, artificial light and reflecting materials change how colour reads.

### 5.3 Metals and finishes
- [REC] One dominant metal finish gives continuity across handles, taps, profiles, hardware and lamps; a secondary finish
  and, exceptionally, extra touches are fine.
- [EST] Matt black, brass, bronze, stainless, chrome and nickel can coexist: it depends on tone, sheen, texture,
  proportion and context. Never ban combinations like stainless with black, brass with chrome or different brushed
  finishes automatically.

### 5.4 Woods
- [EST] No maximum of two woods per room: several work with a visual hierarchy and a conscious relation of temperature,
  undertone, saturation, grain, finish and visual weight.
  - Identify each wood's temperature and undertone.
  - Compare intensity, saturation and grain scale.
  - Set the dominant wood and the secondary ones.
  - Use contrast on purpose: close woods give continuity; very different woods can give positive tension.
- [REC] Avoid woods that are almost but not quite the same, when the difference looks like a matching error.
- [TEC] Respect the grain direction and the real construction of the piece; show continuous grain across kitchen fronts
  only when the product or the design specifies it.

### 5.5 Sensory ingredients per room
Diversity helps; none of these is forced into every room.
| Ingredient | Brings | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Soft | visual and tactile comfort: fabric, wool, linen, velvet, curtain, rug | by use, upkeep, acoustics, language [CTX] |
| Natural | warmth, authenticity: wood, stone, ceramic, fibres, plants | structural, decorative or a texture; not always a plant [EST] |
| Dark | depth and visual anchoring | when the room needs contrast; not required in light palettes [EST] |
| Matt/gloss contrast | variation of light and reading of materials | can be subtle; no artificial gloss to meet a rule [EST] |

### 5.6 Language of forms [EST]
- A dominant formal language creates unity: straight and thin, curved and soft, organic, strong geometries, or
  deliberate combinations. Repeating a motif reinforces the intent, but repeating a form in three places is not required.
- Look for relations: an organic table can talk to a curved mirror and a sofa with rounded arms; a linear kitchen can be
  balanced by a sculptural piece. The aim is visual kinship, not literal uniformity.

### 5.7 Composition and focal point [EST]
- One main focal point per room: a work of art, a view, a fireplace, an island or a piece of furniture.
- Several high-impact pieces must not fight for attention without hierarchy.
- Steer the eye with scale, contrast, light and placement.
- Keep areas of visual rest and negative space.
- Check the composition from the real viewpoints of entering and using the room.

## 7. Compatibility of styles, materials and finishes
Compatibility is not a fixed property of two materials: it comes from the whole composition — undertone, finish,
proportion, scale, light, architectural context and aesthetic intent.

### 7.1 Scale
| Rating | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Compatible | tends to work naturally | a starting point |
| Compatible with conditions | works when tone, proportion, finish or context are controlled | state the condition, check the composition |
| Difficult | needs more care to avoid visual conflict | test alternatives, justify |
| Avoid | a real conflict or it weakens the intent | avoid by default; an exception only with a clear aesthetic intent |

### 7.2 Bridges between styles (examples) [EST]
- Linen and light oak can bridge Nordic and warm contemporary.
- Natural stone, handmade ceramic and wood bring Mediterranean, organic and contemporary languages together.
- Matt black can link industrial, minimalist and contemporary interiors.
- Organic forms and natural materials bring Japandi, warm contemporary and Mediterranean references together.
- Brushed brass adds warmth to contemporary, classic or minimalist rooms when its presence is controlled.

### 7.3 Combinations that need care (examples) [EST]
- Very shiny brass with weathered grey wood can create tension — not incompatible by definition: the metal's tone, the
  amount and the other materials decide.
- Several whites with different undertones side by side can look accidental.
- Woods almost but not quite matching can look like a selection error.
- Mixes of very polished metals, chromes and golds can get noisy without a hierarchy.
- Strong patterns at once compete: limit or rank their scale and number.

### 7.4 Compatibility procedure [REC]
1. Name the aesthetic intent of the combination.
2. Compare undertones, saturation, texture, sheen and scale.
3. Set the dominant element and the accent.
4. Judge it with the room's other materials.
5. Test it under the intended natural and artificial light.
6. Reject or adjust it if it looks accidental or fights the focal point.

## 11. Materials, durability, upkeep, sustainability
A material is chosen by its look and its behaviour in real use: it can look perfect and still be wrong for the use.

### 11.1 Selection criteria [CTX]
- Wear and impact resistance.
- Behaviour with humidity, water, heat, grease and cleaning products.
- Ease of upkeep and repair.
- Durability and how it ages visually.
- Safety, including slip resistance where it applies [TEC].
- Compatibility with adjacent materials and the installation method [TEC].
- Fit for the use, traffic, children or pets.
- Origin, certifications and environmental impact when there is relevant data.

### 11.2 Materials by context [CTX]
- Kitchens: stains, heat, abrasion, cleaning. Bathrooms: humidity, absorption, slip, joints. Traffic zones: wear and
  upkeep. Bedrooms and living rooms: touch, acoustics, cleaning, ageing.

### 11.3 Scale and authenticity of materials [TEC]
- The texture matches the material's real size: boards, slabs, joints, patterns.
- Joints follow the format and system chosen; 2–3 mm is a reference for some finishes, not universal.
- No visibly repeating texture patterns.
- Roughness, sheen and relief match the real finish.
- No ageing, stains or wear without intent and without relation to the material.

### 11.4 Sustainability and longevity [REC]
- Prefer durable, repairable solutions fit for the use over choices that only impress in the image. With similar
  performance, consider certified materials, responsible origin, low upkeep and fewer replacements.
- [OBR] (B3) Never claim certifications or environmental properties without confirmation.
